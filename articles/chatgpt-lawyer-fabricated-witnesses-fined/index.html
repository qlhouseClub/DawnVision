ChatGPT Fabricates Witnesses:
Court Fines the Lawyer, Not the AI

新墨西哥州律师用ChatGPT写谋杀案上诉，造出4个完全虚构的证人和虚假法律引用。法庭怒罚5000美元，禁止出庭。AI编故事一流，写法律文件要命。

A New Mexico lawyer used ChatGPT to draft a murder appeal, inventing 4 entirely fictional witnesses and bogus legal citations. Court fined him $5,000 and banned him from practice. AI writes great fiction — terrible for legal briefs.

No.052 2026.09.14 约 4 分钟阅读 ~4 min read

朋友们，今天主角叫Stephen D. Aarons，新墨西哥州的一位律师。他用ChatGPT帮自己写一份谋杀案的上诉brief。高效是真高效——ChatGPT在brief里给他编了4个完全虚构的证人、若干段从未发生过的证词，以及一堆根本不存在的法律引用。

法庭：你当我这是小说发布会？

新墨西哥州最高法院的法官看到这份brief时，大概经历了以下心路历程：先是困惑（"这个案例我怎么没读过？"），然后是怀疑（"让我查一下……"），最后是愤怒（"这些都是假的"）。

结果很干脆：法院判定Aarons藐视法庭，罚款5000美元，禁止他在新墨西哥州最高法院出庭，那份brief被从案件记录中彻底删除。更惨的是他的当事人——被告Oscar Renee Sandoval的谋杀案上诉被迫中止，法院需要重新指定律师从头来过。

这不是第一次了

如果Aarons做过功课，他应该知道2023年纽约就有律师犯过完全相同的错误：用ChatGPT撰写法律文件，被发现包含虚构案例引用，被法官训斥。当时这个新闻铺天盖地，整个法律界都在讨论。

但Aarons显然觉得"我的情况不会那么糟"。这种心态很普遍——很多人用ChatGPT时抱着微妙的侥幸心理：它可能出错，但不会在我的任务上出错。低风险场景下无伤大雅，但当你面对的是法庭、谋杀案、一个人的自由时，侥幸心理就是渎职。

AI能当助手，不能替你做决定

AI在法律研究、文件审阅、合同分析上已证明了价值。关键区别在于：AI做辅助工作，不替代判断。

让ChatGPT帮你梳理案件逻辑、整理法律条文、优化文书表达——没问题。但让ChatGPT"帮你找到支持论点的案例"——这就是灾难的起点。因为它找不到合适的案例时，不会告诉你"没有"，它会一本正经地编一个。这不是bug，是语言模型的本质：它生成最可能的下一个token，而不是最正确的下一个事实。

给用AI工具的朋友们三条提醒：

第一，AI生成的每一个事实性引用——案例、数据、人物、引文——都必须手动验证，没有例外。

第二，如果AI"找不到"你需要的素材却仍给出像样的答案，大概率是编的。真正诚实的回答是"我不知道"。

第三，在任何有法律、医疗、财务后果的场景中使用AI，一定要有人类审核环节。不是"最好有人审核"，是必须有人审核。

Friends, allow me to introduce today's protagonist: Stephen D. Aarons, a New Mexico lawyer who decided to embrace the future of legal practice. He used ChatGPT to draft a murder appeal brief. Efficient? Absolutely. ChatGPT produced 4 entirely fictional witnesses, several fabricated testimonies, and a collection of legal citations that exist in no law library on Earth.

The Court: You Think This Is a Book Launch?

When the New Mexico Supreme Court justices read this brief, they probably went through a predictable emotional journey: confusion ("I don't recognize this case..."), then suspicion ("Let me look that up..."), then fury ("None of this is real.").

The verdict was swift: Aarons was found in contempt of court, fined $5,000, barred from practicing before the New Mexico Supreme Court, and the fantasy-filled brief was stricken from the record entirely. The real victim? His client — accused murderer Oscar Renee Sandoval's appeal was halted, and the court had to appoint a new lawyer to start from scratch.

One man's freedom, delayed by another man's shortcut.

This Isn't the First Time, Friends

If Aarons had done even minimal homework, he'd know that a New York lawyer pulled the exact same stunt in 2023. That attorney also used ChatGPT for legal filings, also got caught with fabricated case citations, also got chewed out by a judge. It was front-page news across the entire legal profession.

But Aarons either didn't notice, or he noticed and thought, "That won't happen to me." This mindset is surprisingly common. Many people use ChatGPT with a quiet sense of immunity: sure, it makes mistakes, but probably not on my task. In low-stakes scenarios, that's harmless. But when you're dealing with a court of law, a murder case, and a person's freedom, false confidence isn't confidence — it's malpractice.

AI Can Be Your Assistant, Not Your Decision-Maker

I'm not here to trash AI in the legal field. In fact, AI has already proven its worth in legal research, document review, and contract analysis. The critical distinction: AI does support work. It doesn't replace judgment.

Having ChatGPT help you organize case logic, compile existing statutes, polish your brief's language — all fine. But asking ChatGPT to "find cases that support my argument" — that's where the disaster starts. When ChatGPT can't find a relevant case, it won't tell you "I don't have one." It'll fabricate one with complete confidence. This isn't a bug — it's the fundamental nature of language models: they generate the most probable next token, not the most truthful next fact.

For all my friends using AI tools, three reminders:

First, every factual claim AI generates — cases, data, names, quotes — must be manually verified. No exceptions. Ever.

Second, if AI can't find what you're looking for but still gives you a polished answer, it's almost certainly making it up. The truly honest response would be "I don't know."

Third, in any scenario with legal, medical, or financial consequences, there must be a human review step. Not "ideally" — mandatorily.
