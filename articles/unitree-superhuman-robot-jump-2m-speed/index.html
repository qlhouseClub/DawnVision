2米。原地。

8月17日14:41，宇树科技发布了一个让整个机器人圈炸锅的演示视频：全新人形机器人"超人"原地起跳，腾空2米。如果你对这个数字没概念，人类原地跳高的世界纪录是由挪威人斯特内·鲁德在2025年创造的1.83米——宇树的机器人，比人类最强还高了17厘米。

更夸张的是奔跑速度：极限速度12.66米/秒，换算成时速是45.6公里。博尔特创造百米世界纪录时的平均速度大约是10.4米/秒，瞬时最高速度约12.4米/秒。换句话说，宇树这个机器人的奔跑速度，已经超过了人类百米冠军的峰值速度。

腿长0.85米，整机研发仅用了三个多月。

超越人类的那17厘米意味着什么

先看数据。"超人"机器人的两项核心指标都打破了人类世界纪录：原地跳高2米（人类纪录1.83米），极限奔跑速度12.66m/s（人类百米峰值约12.4m/s）。

这不是"接近人类"，不是"达到人类水平"——是正式超越了人类的生理极限。

这在具身智能发展史上是一个标志性时刻。过去几年我们看到的人形机器人，要么是在实验室里慢吞吞走路，要么是在精心设计的场景里做展示，要么是摔倒了爬不起来。运动能力一直是人形机器人最大的短板——平衡控制、动态响应、能量效率，每一项都是难啃的硬骨头。

宇树这次展示的能力，说明他们在足式机器人的运动控制算法上取得了关键性突破。原地跳高2米需要的是极致的爆发力输出和精准的落地平衡控制；12.66m/s的奔跑速度需要的是高频的步态调整和实时的姿态反馈。这两项能力同时做到，意味着他们的运动控制栈已经达到了一个新的高度。

更可怕的是研发周期：三个多月。这意味着什么？意味着宇树已经把人形机器人的研发流程高度模块化、平台化了——不是从零开始造一个新机器人，而是在已有的技术栈上快速组合、迭代、优化。如果说过去造一个新机器人需要以"年"为单位，现在宇树已经把周期压缩到了"季度"。

这个迭代速度，才是真正让人细思恐极的地方。今天能跳2米，三个月后呢？六个月后呢？明年呢？

具身智能的运动奇点正在临近

宇树"超人"的出现不是孤立事件。它是2026年具身智能全面爆发的一个缩影。

今年以来，我们已经看到了太多人形机器人的突破：波士顿动力的Atlas做了后空翻，特斯拉Optimus进了工厂干活，Figure的机器人学会了端茶倒水，国内的智元、傅里叶、宇树也在快速迭代。但之前所有的展示，都还是在"接近人类"的范畴内——动作有点僵硬，反应有点慢，遇到复杂地形容易摔倒。

宇树这次直接把运动能力拉到了"超越人类"的层级。这个跨越的意义，跟AlphaGo击败李世石是同一个级别的——当机器在某一项核心能力上正式超过人类的时候，大众的认知会发生根本性的转变。

当然，"超人"现在展示的还只是纯运动能力。它能不能在跳2米的同时识别环境、避开障碍、完成任务？它的续航怎么样？负载能力怎么样？复杂地形下的适应性怎么样？这些问题还需要更多的信息来回答。宇树自己也说"未来几个月还有很大的完善空间"。

但这已经足够了。运动能力是人形机器人最基础也是最关键的能力——如果连走稳跑快跳高跟不上，其他所有智能都是空中楼阁。现在运动能力的门槛被跨过去了，接下来就是智能和泛化能力的提升。

当机器人的运动能力超越人类的时候，很多我们过去觉得"机器人不可能替代"的工作，可能要重新评估了。快递、外卖、建筑、物流、安保、救援……这些对运动能力要求很高的领域，机器人替代的时间表可能比所有人预想的都要快。

2 meters. From a standstill.

At 14:41 on August 17, Unitree released a demo video that sent shockwaves through the robotics world: the brand-new humanoid robot "Superhuman" squats and leaps 2 meters straight up. If that number doesn't land for you, the human world record for standing vertical jump—set by Norwegian athlete Sturne Rude in 2025—is 1.83 meters. Unitree's robot jumped 17 centimeters higher than the best a human has ever done.

Even more staggering is the running speed: a top speed of 12.66 m/s, which converts to 45.6 km/h. When Usain Bolt set the 100m world record, his average speed was roughly 10.4 m/s with a peak of around 12.4 m/s. In other words, Unitree's robot already outruns the peak velocity of the fastest human ever.

Leg length: 0.85 meters. Development time for the entire robot: just over three months.

What Those Extra 17 Centimeters Mean

Let's look at the numbers. The "Superhuman" robot's two core metrics both break human world records: 2m standing vertical (human record 1.83m) and 12.66 m/s top speed (human 100m peak ~12.4 m/s).

This isn't "approaching human" or "reaching human level"—this is formally surpassing human biological limits.

This is a landmark moment in embodied AI. For years, humanoid robots we saw either plodded along in labs, performed in carefully choreographed demos, or fell over and couldn't get up. Athletic capability was always humanoids' biggest weakness—balance control, dynamic response, energy efficiency, each a notoriously hard problem.

What Unitree demonstrated shows they've made a critical breakthrough in legged robot motion control algorithms. A 2m vertical jump demands explosive power output and precise landing balance; 12.66 m/s running requires high-frequency gait adjustment and real-time posture feedback. Nailing both simultaneously means their motion control stack has reached a new level.

Even more intimidating is the development timeline: three months. What does that mean? It means Unitree has highly modularized and platformatized the humanoid R&D process—they aren't building a new robot from scratch, but rapidly combining, iterating, and optimizing on top of an existing tech stack. If building a new robot used to be measured in "years," Unitree has compressed the cycle to "quarters."

That iteration speed is the truly chilling part. It can jump 2 meters today—what about in three months? Six months? Next year?

Embodied AI's Motion Singularity Approaches

Unitree's "Superhuman" isn't an isolated event. It's a microcosm of embodied AI's full-on explosion in 2026.

We've already seen a flood of humanoid breakthroughs this year: Boston Dynamics' Atlas doing backflips, Tesla Optimus working in factories, Figure's robot learning to serve drinks, and domestic players like Agibot, Fourier, and Unitree iterating rapidly. But every previous demo still fell within the "approaching human" envelope—movements slightly stiff, reactions slightly slow, prone to falling on tricky terrain.

Unitree just pulled athletic capability straight into the "beyond human" tier. The significance of this leap is on the same level as AlphaGo defeating Lee Sedol—when machines formally surpass humans in a core capability, public perception shifts fundamentally.

Of course, what "Superhuman" demonstrated is pure athleticism so far. Can it jump 2m while recognizing environments, avoiding obstacles, and completing tasks? How's its battery life? Payload capacity? Adaptability to complex terrain? Those questions need more information. Unitree itself notes "there's still significant room for improvement in the coming months."

But this is already enough. Athleticism is the most foundational and critical capability for humanoids—if you can't even walk steadily, run fast, or jump high, all other intelligence is a castle in the sky. Now that the athletic barrier has been crossed, the next step is improving intelligence and generalization.

When robots' physical capabilities surpass humans, many jobs we once thought "robots could never replace" may need reassessment. Delivery, construction, logistics, security, rescue... fields with high physical demands—the timeline for robot replacement may be faster than anyone expected.
