SpaceX Earnings: AI Revenue Surges 247%
$170M Daily Spend Sends Stock Tumbling

Q2总营收78亿美元超预期，AI收入25.6亿美元占三分之一，但158亿美元季度AI资本开支吓坏市场，盘后跌8%。

Q2 revenue $7.8B beats expectations; AI revenue hits $2.56B at one-third of total, but $15.8B quarterly AI capex terrifies markets, sending stock down 8% after hours.

No.030 2026.08.06 约 5 分钟阅读 ~5 min read

8月4日盘后，马斯克交出了SpaceX上市后的第一份季度成绩单。数字很炸裂，市场反应却很微妙。

先看好消息：Q2总营收78.14亿美元，同比增长92%，大幅超出市场预期的69亿美元；调整后EBITDA首次转正（Q1还是-6亿美元）；AI业务收入25.61亿美元，同比暴增247%，占总营收约三分之一。Starlink用户数突破700万，发射业务继续保持全球第一。

再看坏消息：季度AI资本开支约158亿美元，平均每天在AI上砸1.7亿美元。马斯克明确表示SpaceX"只用英伟达GPU"，Colossus数据中心还在疯狂扩建。财报发布后，SpaceX盘后股价下跌7-8%——营收超预期，但市场被百亿美元级别的烧钱速度吓坏了。

卖GPU算力，比发射火箭更赚钱？

这份财报最有意思的地方不是火箭发射，而是SpaceX的身份正在发生根本性转变：它正在从一家"火箭公司"变成一家"AI算力公司"。

AI业务25.6亿美元的季度收入是怎么来的？答案是Colossus数据中心的算力转售。SpaceX在得克萨斯州建设的Colossus超算集群，目前已经是全球最大的GPU集群之一（超过10万块H100/B200），主要客户是Anthropic、xAI以及其他需要大规模算力的AI公司。简单来说：SpaceX买英伟达的GPU，建数据中心，然后把GPU小时卖给AI公司——本质上是个"算力批发商"。

这个生意有多赚钱？看毛利率就知道了。火箭发射业务的毛利率大约在20-30%（因为火箭制造成本、燃料、发射场运营都很贵），Starlink的毛利率在40-50%左右（卫星折旧是主要成本），而算力转售业务的毛利率据说能达到60-70%——一旦数据中心建起来，后续的主要成本就是电费和运维，边际成本很低。难怪马斯克要在AI上砸这么多钱，这确实是比发射火箭更好的生意。

马斯克自己也承认："我们没想到卖GPU算力会这么赚钱。"Colossus一期刚上线就被Anthropic等客户包圆了，二期、三期还在建设中，预订已经排到了2028年。AI算力现在是卖方市场，只要你有电、有GPU、能快速交付，客户排队给你送钱。

1.7亿美元一天：市场在怕什么？

既然AI算力这么赚钱，为什么财报发布后股价反而跌了？因为市场看到了一个让人脊背发凉的数字：158亿美元的季度AI资本开支。

"当一家公司每天在GPU上花1.7亿美元，你第一个想到的问题不是'它能赚多少钱'，而是'这个泡沫什么时候破'。"—— 一位华尔街科技分析师

158亿美元是什么概念？这比很多Fortune 500强公司一整年的营收还高。这是单季度的资本开支，不是年度的。按这个烧钱速度，SpaceX一年在AI上的投入超过600亿美元——相当于OpenAI全年营收的好几倍。

市场的担忧主要有三个。

第一，投入产出比的不确定性。现在AI算力确实供不应求，但这个供需失衡能持续多久？如果未来2-3年大量数据中心建成、GPU产能上来、或者算法效率大幅提升（比如小模型效果追平大模型），算力价格可能会像云服务价格一样持续下跌。今天砸几百亿美元建的数据中心，3年后会不会变成昂贵的废铁？

第二，客户集中度风险。SpaceX的算力收入高度依赖少数几个大客户（Anthropic是最大客户，据传xAI也在用）。如果Anthropic未来转用其他供应商、或者自己建数据中心、或者融资烧完了，SpaceX的算力收入可能会断崖式下跌。单一客户占比过高，这是所有B2B基础设施生意的大忌。

第三，马斯克的管理精力分散。马斯克现在同时管着Tesla、SpaceX、xAI、Neuralink、The Boring Company、Twitter/X六家公司，其中SpaceX和xAI都在疯狂烧钱建数据中心。一个人的精力是有限的，同时管这么多百亿美元级别的超级项目，会不会出问题？历史上很少有企业家能同时管好两家千亿美元公司，更别说六家。

但马斯克显然不这么想。他在财报电话会上的表态非常激进："AI算力是21世纪的石油，现在的投入不是太多，而是太少。我们会继续加速建设Colossus，未来四个季度的AI资本开支可能还会增加。"

这就是典型的马斯克式豪赌：要么all in赢者通吃，要么赌输了一起完蛋。华尔街喜欢这种故事，但也害怕这种故事。2000年互联网泡沫的时候，电信公司也是这样疯狂砸钱建光缆、建数据中心，结果泡沫一破，90%的产能闲置，股价跌去90%以上。AI算力会不会重蹈覆辙？现在没人知道。

但有一点是确定的：SpaceX已经不是你认识的那个只造火箭的公司了。它现在是AI算力军备竞赛里最激进的玩家之一。

明天见。

After hours on August 4, Elon Musk delivered SpaceX's first quarterly report card since going public. The numbers are explosive, but the market reaction was nuanced.

First the good news: Q2 total revenue hit $7.814 billion, up 92% year-over-year, significantly beating consensus estimates of ~$6.9 billion; adjusted EBITDA turned positive for the first time (Q1 was -$600 million); AI business revenue reached $2.561 billion, surging 247% YoY and accounting for roughly one-third of total revenue. Starlink subscribers passed 7 million; launch business remains global number one.

Now the bad news: quarterly AI capital expenditure was approximately $15.8 billion, averaging $170 million per day spent on AI. Musk explicitly stated SpaceX uses "only NVIDIA GPUs," and the Colossus data center is still expanding furiously. After the earnings release, SpaceX stock dropped 7–8% after hours — revenue beat, but the market was terrified by the pace of spending in the tens of billions.

Selling GPU Compute Is More Profitable Than Launching Rockets?

The most interesting thing about this earnings report isn't rocket launches; it's that SpaceX's identity is fundamentally shifting: it's going from a "rocket company" to an "AI compute company."

Where does $2.56 billion in quarterly AI revenue come from? The answer is compute resale from the Colossus data center. SpaceX's Colossus supercomputing cluster in Texas is already one of the world's largest GPU clusters (over 100,000 H100/B200 GPUs), with primary customers being Anthropic, xAI, and other AI companies needing massive scale. Simply put: SpaceX buys NVIDIA GPUs, builds data centers, and resells GPU hours to AI companies — essentially a "compute wholesaler."

How profitable is this business? Gross margins tell the story. Rocket launch gross margins run around 20–30% (rocket manufacturing, fuel, launch pad operations are expensive); Starlink gross margins are around 40–50% (satellite depreciation is the main cost); compute resale gross margins are reportedly 60–70% — once the data center is built, ongoing costs are mainly electricity and operations with very low marginal cost. No wonder Musk is pouring this much money into AI; it really is a better business than launching rockets.

Musk himself admitted: "We didn't expect selling GPU compute would be this profitable." Colossus Phase 1 was fully booked by Anthropic and other customers immediately upon going live; Phases 2 and 3 are still under construction with reservations stretching to 2028. AI compute is currently a seller's market — if you have power, GPUs, and can deliver fast, customers are lined up throwing money at you.

$170 Million Per Day: What Is the Market Afraid Of?

If AI compute is this profitable, why did the stock drop after earnings? Because the market saw a spine-chilling number: $15.8 billion in quarterly AI capex.

"When a company is spending $170 million a day on GPUs, the first question you ask isn't 'how much money can it make,' it's 'when does this bubble pop.'"— A Wall Street tech analyst

What does $15.8 billion mean? It's more than the full-year revenue of many Fortune 500 companies. That's quarterly capex, not annual. At this burn rate, SpaceX is spending over $60 billion a year on AI — multiple times OpenAI's full-year revenue.

The market has three main concerns.

First, uncertainty around ROI. AI compute is indeed in short supply right now, but how long will this supply-demand imbalance last? If massive data center capacity comes online over the next 2–3 years, GPU production ramps up, or algorithm efficiency improves dramatically (e.g., small models catching up to large ones), compute prices could decline steadily the way cloud services prices have. Could the data centers being built for tens of billions today become expensive scrap metal three years from now?

Second, customer concentration risk. SpaceX's compute revenue depends heavily on a small number of large customers (Anthropic is the largest, with xAI reportedly also a major user). If Anthropic switches to other suppliers in the future, builds its own data centers, or burns through its funding, SpaceX's compute revenue could cliff-dive. Over-reliance on a single customer is a cardinal sin in any B2B infrastructure business.

Third, Musk's stretched management bandwidth. Musk is currently running Tesla, SpaceX, xAI, Neuralink, The Boring Company, and Twitter/X simultaneously — six companies, with SpaceX and xAI both burning cash building data centers at a ferocious pace. A person's bandwidth is finite; running this many multi-billion-dollar mega-projects simultaneously — how can it not go wrong? History shows few entrepreneurs can successfully run two $100B+ companies concurrently, let alone six.

But Musk clearly disagrees. His tone on the earnings call was aggressive: "AI compute is the oil of the 21st century. We're not spending too much — we're spending too little. We will continue accelerating Colossus construction; AI capex over the next four quarters may actually increase."

This is classic Musk-style all-in: bet everything for winner-take-all, or go down together. Wall Street loves this narrative but also fears it. During the dot-com bubble in 2000, telecom companies were similarly pouring money into fiber optic cables and data centers, and when the bubble popped, 90% of capacity went idle and stocks lost 90%+ of their value. Will AI compute repeat history? Nobody knows right now.

But one thing is certain: SpaceX is no longer the company you knew that only built rockets. It's now one of the most aggressive players in the AI compute arms race.
