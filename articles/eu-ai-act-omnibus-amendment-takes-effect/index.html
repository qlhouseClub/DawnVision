EU AI Act Amendment Takes Effect Today
High-Risk AI Delayed + Power Centralized

The Digital Omnibus on AI, taking effect July 27, delays high-risk AI applicability but expands the Commission's rule coordination, quasi-legislative, and centralized enforcement powers. Regulation isn't loosening — it's centralizing.

今天，2026年7月27日，欧盟的AI监管规则又一次变了。

7月24日在《欧盟官方公报》正式公布的《人工智能法案数字综合修订案》（Digital Omnibus on AI），于今日正式生效。这份修订案的核心动作可以用一句话概括：给企业更多时间，但把权力收得更紧。

推迟不等于放松：高风险AI延后的真相

最引人注目的变化是高风险AI系统的适用时间被推迟了。

独立型高风险AI（standalone）：从原定时间推迟到2027年12月2日起适用。嵌入型高风险AI（embedded，比如嵌入医疗器械、汽车里的AI）：推迟到2028年8月2日起适用。前后加起来，企业多争取了大约12到18个月的缓冲期。

表面上看，这是监管的松绑——给行业更多时间准备。但仔细看就会发现，时间推迟了，规则的覆盖面和执行力度反而增强了。

怎么理解这个逻辑？想象一下：如果监管的目标是“尽快让规则落地”，那应该按期执行甚至提前。但如果监管的目标是“制定一套真正能管得住、管得好的规则”，那多花点时间把规则磨细、把执行机制建全，反而意味着监管会更严、更持久。欧盟不是要放AI一马，而是要确保出手的时候，一击必中。

权力上收：欧委会拿到了四张新王牌

修订案真正重要的变化，不是推迟了多长时间，而是欧委会（European Commission）拿到了哪些新权力。四张王牌，张张都指向同一个方向：监管权力从成员国向欧盟层面集中。

第一张牌：强化规则协调权。欧委会可以通过实施法案，协调《AI法案》与机械法规、医疗器械法规等部门法之间的适用关系，减少规则重叠。翻译成人话：AI和其他领域的监管如果打架了，欧委会说了算。这解决了过去AI监管“九龙治水”的问题，但也意味着欧委会的话语权大大增强。

第二张牌：扩大准立法权。欧委会可以依据《机械法规》通过授权法案增设AI健康与安全要求。简单说：欧委会可以不经过议会完整立法程序，直接给AI加新的安全要求。这是一种“准立法”的权力——效率高，但民主监督相对弱。

第三张牌：增强集中执法权。明确欧盟AI Office对通用人工智能模型相关系统的监督权限，推动AI监管由成员国分散执法向欧盟层面集中。以前每个成员国各管各的，执法标准不统一，企业可以“挑软柿子捏”。现在权力收归欧盟AI Office，全欧盟一个标准、一支队伍，执法力度会显著加强。

第四张牌：强化软法解释权。欧委会被要求发布指导意见，协调AI法案与部门立法的适用。“指导意见”听起来软，但在欧盟的法律体系里，这些指导意见往往就是实际执行的依据——企业和监管机构都会照着做。

四张牌合在一起，画出来的图景很清晰：AI监管的决策重心正在从“27个成员国各管各的”转向“布鲁塞尔统一指挥”。对企业来说，好消息是不用面对27套不同的规则了；坏消息是，那一套统一的规则，可能比任何一个成员国单独制定的都更严。

Today, July 27, 2026, the EU's AI regulatory rulebook changes again.

The Digital Omnibus on AI, formally published in the Official Journal of the European Union on July 24, takes effect today. The core of this amendment can be summed up in one sentence: more time for companies, but tighter power at the center.

Delay Doesn't Mean Relaxation: The Truth About High-Risk AI Postponement

The most eye-catching change is that the applicability timeline for high-risk AI systems has been pushed back.

Standalone high-risk AI: delayed to December 2, 2027. Embedded high-risk AI (like AI in medical devices or automobiles): delayed to August 2, 2028. All told, companies have gained roughly 12 to 18 months of breathing room.

On the surface, this looks like regulatory loosening — giving the industry more time to prepare. But look closer and you'll find that while the timeline has been pushed back, the scope and enforcement of the rules have actually grown stronger.

How to make sense of this logic? Imagine: if the goal of regulation were “get rules in place as soon as possible,” you'd enforce on schedule or even early. But if the goal is “build a set of rules that actually work and actually hold up,” then spending more time refining the rules and building out enforcement mechanisms means regulation will be stricter and more durable. The EU isn't going easy on AI — it's making sure that when it strikes, it strikes decisively.

Power Flows Upward: The Commission Gets Four New Trump Cards

The truly important change in the amendment isn't how long the delay is — it's what new powers the European Commission has gained. Four trump cards, all pointing in the same direction: regulatory power is centralizing from member states to the EU level.

Card one: strengthened rule coordination power. The Commission can coordinate the application of the AI Act with sectoral legislation like machinery and medical device regulations through implementing acts, reducing overlap. In plain language: if AI regulation conflicts with other regulatory domains, the Commission decides. This solves the old “too many cooks” problem of AI regulation, but it also means the Commission's voice gets much louder.

Card two: expanded quasi-legislative power. The Commission can add new AI health and safety requirements through delegated acts under the Machinery Regulation. Put simply: the Commission can impose new safety requirements on AI without going through the full parliamentary legislative process. This is “quasi-legislative” power — efficient, but with relatively weaker democratic oversight.

Card three: enhanced centralized enforcement. The EU AI Office gets explicit oversight authority over general-purpose AI model systems, pushing AI regulation from fragmented member-state enforcement toward EU-level centralization. Previously, each member state did its own thing with inconsistent enforcement standards, and companies could “pick the softest target.” Now power converges at the EU AI Office — one standard, one team, across the whole bloc. Enforcement intensity will increase significantly.

Card four: strengthened soft-law interpretation. The Commission is required to issue guidance coordinating the AI Act with sectoral legislation. “Guidance” sounds soft, but in the EU legal system, these guidance documents often become the de facto basis for enforcement — both companies and regulators follow them.

Put the four cards together, and the picture is clear: the decision-making center of AI regulation is shifting from “27 member states each doing their own thing” to “unified command from Brussels.” For companies, the good news is no more 27 different rulebooks. The bad news? That one unified rulebook might be stricter than any single member state would have written on its own.
