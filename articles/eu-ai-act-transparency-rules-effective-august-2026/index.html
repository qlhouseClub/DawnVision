EU AI Act Transparency Rules Take Effect
AI Regulation Enters Enforcement Era

8月2日，欧盟AI法首批透明度条款正式生效并启动执法——AI生成内容必须标识、聊天机器人必须告知用户。这是全球首部综合性AI监管法律落地的第一刀。

On August 2, the first batch of EU AI Act transparency provisions officially took effect and enforcement began — AI-generated content must be labeled, chatbots must inform users. The first cut of the world's first comprehensive AI regulatory law lands.

8月2日起，你在欧洲用聊天机器人对话、看AI生成的图片或视频、听AI配音的内容，理论上都应该看到明确的AI标识。

这不是建议，是法律义务。欧盟《人工智能法》（AI Act）的首批透明度条款，在经过漫长的立法和准备期后，于2026年8月2日正式生效并启动执法。这是全球第一部综合性AI监管法律从"纸面"走向"落地"的第一刀——先管最容易执行的透明度，再逐步推进更深层次的合规要求。

透明度规则管什么？谁要负责？

这次生效的透明度规则，核心要求很明确：用户有权知道自己在和AI打交道。

具体来说，几类场景必须明确标识：第一，通用AI聊天机器人（比如ChatGPT、Claude、Gemini的网页版和App），必须让用户清楚知道自己在和AI对话；第二，AI生成的内容（文字、图片、音频、视频），发布者必须标注是AI生成的；第三，AI生成的深度伪造内容，必须有清晰的水印或标识；第四，情感识别和生物特征分类系统，也需要告知被识别者。

谁来负责？不是模型公司一家的事——模型提供者和内容发布者都有责任。模型公司需要在产品中内置标识功能，内容平台和发布者需要确保AI生成的内容被正确标注。欧盟委员会的AI办公室和各成员国的主管部门已经同步启动执法，意味着这不是摆样子，是真的会开罚单的。

那罚得重不重？违反AI法的最高罚款可以达到全球营业额的7%，比GDPR的4%还高。当然，透明度条款作为第一批生效的义务，初期执法可能以警告和整改为主，但罚款的达摩克利斯之剑是悬着的——对于大平台来说，7%的全球营业额可能就是几十亿欧元。

为什么先管透明度？监管的"从易到难"策略

欧盟AI法采用的是"分期生效"策略，这很聪明。

整部AI法的合规义务不是一刀切全部生效的，而是按照风险等级和执行难度分批落地：8月先生效的是透明度规则（通用AI和深度伪造的标识），社会共识最高、技术上最容易执行；然后2027年12月生效高风险AI系统的合规要求（招聘、教育、执法、医疗这些领域的AI系统），执行难度大很多；最后2028年生效嵌入实体产品的AI系统（比如机器人、自动驾驶里的AI），技术和监管难度最大。

这种"先易后难"的策略有两个好处：第一，让监管机构和企业都有一个适应期，不会一下子被合规要求压垮；第二，用透明度这个"最容易达成共识"的规则，先把AI监管的合法性和权威性建立起来，后面推进更难的规则时阻力会小一些。

"透明度是AI监管的第一道门槛——不是因为它最重要，而是因为它最容易执行。先立住了，后面的规则才能推进。"—— 一位AI政策研究者

对于出海欧洲的中国企业来说，这波透明度合规是一个必须过的坎。做AI内容的平台、做AI客服的服务商、做大模型应用的产品，都需要检查自己的AI标识是否到位。不要小看这个看似简单的要求——很多产品的AI交互设计就是刻意让用户感觉不到是AI在对话，现在法律要求必须明确标识，产品设计逻辑可能需要调整。

当然，挑战也很大。AI生成内容的传播链条太长了——模型生成、平台发布、用户转发、二次创作，每一步都可能丢失AI标识。全链条追溯几乎不可能，监管最终能执行到什么程度，还要打个问号。但方向是确定的：AI内容的透明化和可追溯，是全球监管的大趋势。

欧盟AI法的透明度规则生效，标志着AI监管真正进入了执法时代。以前大家讨论AI监管都是"未来会不会管"，现在答案很明确：已经开始管了，而且会越来越严。

明天见。

Starting August 2nd, when you chat with a chatbot, view AI-generated images or videos, or listen to AI-dubbed content in Europe — in theory, you should see clear AI labeling.

This isn't a recommendation — it's a legal obligation. The first batch of transparency provisions in the EU's Artificial Intelligence Act officially took effect and enforcement began on August 2, 2026, after a long legislative and preparation period. This is the first cut of the world's first comprehensive AI regulatory law moving from "paper" to "reality" — start with the most enforceable transparency rules, then gradually push for deeper compliance requirements.

What Do the Transparency Rules Cover? Who's Responsible?

The core requirement of the transparency rules taking effect now is clear: users have the right to know they're interacting with AI.

Specifically, several categories of scenarios require clear labeling: first, general-purpose AI chatbots (like the web and app versions of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) must make it clear to users that they're talking to AI; second, AI-generated content (text, images, audio, video) must be labeled as AI-generated by publishers; third, AI-generated deepfakes must have clear watermarks or labels; fourth, emotion recognition and biometric classification systems also need to inform those being identified.

Who's responsible? It's not just model companies — both model providers and content publishers share responsibility. Model companies need to build labeling features into their products; content platforms and publishers need to ensure AI-generated content is properly marked. The European Commission's AI Office and member state authorities have launched enforcement simultaneously — meaning this isn't just for show; fines will actually be issued.

How heavy are the fines? The maximum penalty for violating the AI Act can reach 7% of global turnover — higher than GDPR's 4%. Of course, as the first batch of obligations to take effect, transparency enforcement may start with warnings and corrective actions, but the Damoclean sword of fines is hanging there — for big platforms, 7% of global turnover could be billions of euros.

Why Transparency First? A "From Easy to Hard" Regulatory Strategy

The EU AI Act uses a "phased implementation" strategy — and it's a smart one.

The compliance obligations of the entire AI Act aren't taking effect all at once. They're rolling out in batches based on risk level and enforcement difficulty: transparency rules take effect first in August (labeling for general AI and deepfakes) — highest social consensus, technically easiest to enforce; then compliance requirements for high-risk AI systems (AI in hiring, education, law enforcement, healthcare) take effect in December 2027 — much harder to implement; finally, AI systems embedded in physical products (like robots, autonomous driving AI) take effect in 2028 — the greatest technical and regulatory difficulty.

This "easy first, hard later" strategy has two benefits: first, it gives both regulators and companies an adjustment period, so no one gets crushed by compliance requirements all at once; second, using transparency — the "most consensual" rule — to establish the legitimacy and authority of AI regulation first, so pushing harder rules later encounters less resistance.

"Transparency is the first threshold of AI regulation — not because it's the most important, but because it's the easiest to enforce. Establish it first, and the rest of the rules can follow."— An AI Policy Researcher

For Chinese companies expanding into Europe, this wave of transparency compliance is a hurdle that must be cleared. Platforms creating AI content, service providers doing AI customer service, products building LLM applications — all need to check whether their AI labeling is up to scratch. Don't underestimate this seemingly simple requirement — many AI interaction designs are deliberately crafted to make users feel like they're talking to a human. Now the law requires explicit labeling, and product design logic may need adjustment.

Of course, challenges remain significant. The dissemination chain of AI-generated content is too long — model generation, platform publishing, user sharing, secondary creation — AI labeling can get lost at every step. Full-chain traceability is nearly impossible, and how effectively regulators can ultimately enforce it remains an open question. But the direction is clear: transparency and traceability of AI content is the global regulatory trend.

The EU AI Act's transparency rules taking effect marks the moment AI regulation truly enters the enforcement era. Before, discussions of AI regulation were always about "will they ever actually regulate it?" Now the answer is clear: they already have started, and it's only going to get stricter.
