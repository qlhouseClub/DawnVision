"纯·人工·智能"保洁机器人
200元/小时雇了个遥控的

"Pure Human-Operated Intelligence" Cleaning Robot
$30/Hour for a Remote-Controlled Unit

美国初创Tau Robotics推出人形机器人上门保洁，30美元/小时，能洗手能拖地能捡垃圾——结果评论区一句话让所有人冷静：全是遥控的。这可能是今年AI圈最诚实也最讽刺的产品。

US startup Tau Robotics launches humanoid robot home cleaning at $30/hour. It washes hands, mops floors, picks up trash — then one comment brings everyone back to earth: it's all remote-controlled. Possibly 2026's most honest and most ironic AI product.

No.027 2026.08.03 约 4 分钟阅读 ~4 min read

朋友们，今天AI圈的行为艺术，我必须给你们讲讲。

旧金山一家叫Tau Robotics的初创公司，做了一个人形机器人上门保洁服务。Demo视频拍得那叫一个丝滑：机器人从面包车上下来，穿着深色工装走进你家，挤洗手液洗手、关水龙头、拿擦手巾擦干——全套动作行云流水。拖地的时候看见地上有垃圾，会弯腰捡起来，转身走两步扔进垃圾桶。网友评论："这就是妈妈说的'眼里有活'啊！"

长得也很有记忆点——脑袋顶一个Wi-Fi路由器，酷似天线宝宝，所以国内网友给它起了个外号叫"天线宝宝机器人"。价格也挺亲民，30美元/小时（约200元人民币），在美国真人保洁一小时五六十刀的市场里，这个价格确实有竞争力。

三个机器人"员工"各有分工：Chelsea负责厨卫清洁，Elon负责定期上门保洁（据说来几次就记住你家东西放哪了），Tony主打深度清洁，专治踢脚线、瓷砖缝、冰箱底下等陈年老垢。官网背景还无限循环播放机器人下车的帅气视频，自信满满。

然后，翻到评论区，一句话给所有人浇了盆冷水：

"上面这些，全是遥控的。纯·人工·智能。"

等等，这居然是个聪明的办法？

说实话，看到"全是遥控的"这几个字的时候，我心凉了半截——合着我看了半天，就是一个人戴着VR设备在远处操控机器人干活？这也能叫AI？

但冷静下来想了想，我发现这事没那么简单。Tau Robotics是一家2024年成立的小公司，不到10个人，去年9月拿了300万美元Pre-seed之后就一直没声音。他们不是在骗投资人——官网上虽然没明说遥控，但也没吹"完全自主"。而且有一个细节我必须表扬：所有视频全部是一倍速播放。

朋友们，你们可能不知道，市面上绝绝绝大多数人形机器人家庭demo，两倍加速是起步价，四倍速才勉强能看。Tau敢用一倍速放视频，至少在诚实度上已经赢了90%的同行。

更深层的逻辑其实是数据飞轮。遥操不是偷懒，是一种类似自动驾驶"影子模式"的策略：先让人通过遥控把真实家庭场景跑通，收集大量操作数据，等数据足够了再训练自主模型。去年1X Neo也是这个路子，只不过发布9个月了还没大规模交付。

而且做人形而非轮式底盘，一个很第一性原理的原因是：让人遥操人形机器人，操作员可以用自己的身体直觉直接映射动作，门槛比遥操轮式底盘低得多。人形的形状虽然在"干活效率"上未必最优，但在"数据采集效率"上反而是最优的。

花200元请个人远程帮你拖地，这合理吗？

但问题来了：消费者愿不愿意花30美元一小时，雇一个头顶Wi-Fi路由器的遥控机器人来家里干活？而且这个机器人全程连着网，等于一个带摄像头的遥控设备在你家里走来走去——你擦桌子它看着，你拖地它看着，你家冰箱上贴的账单它都看着。隐私怎么算？

有网友说得好："一个人形的机器在你家走来走去擦桌子倒垃圾，你坐在沙发上看着……花30美元体验一把雅典公民的生活，好像也不是不行。"但玩笑归玩笑，具身智能进家庭的难度确实比大多数人想象的大得多。国内自变量和58同城合作的智能保洁，机器人旁边还得配个真人保洁盯着——不是技术不想自主，是真实家庭环境太复杂了。

最后说一句：我其实挺佩服Tau Robotics的。他们没有做一个炫技的自主demo然后融一大笔钱慢慢烧，而是选择了一个看起来"很笨"但实际可落地的方式——用遥操先把服务跑起来，收客户的钱，积累真实数据。"纯·人工·智能"是网友给的封号，但换个角度看，这可能是当下具身智能创业最诚实的路径。

毕竟，在这个所有机器人demo都四倍速播放、所有AI产品都吹"完全自主"的时代，愿意用一倍速放视频、靠真人遥控先把活干了的公司，反而显得格外可爱。

今天就槽到这里，明天继续。

Friends, I have to tell you about today's performance art in the AI world.

A San Francisco startup called Tau Robotics launched a humanoid robot home cleaning service. The demo videos are buttery smooth: the robot steps out of a van in dark coveralls, walks into your home, dispenses hand soap, washes its hands, turns off the faucet, wipes dry with a towel — fluid as can be. While mopping, it spots trash on the floor, bends down to pick it up, turns, and tosses it in the bin. Commenters said: "That's what moms mean by 'having eyes for work'!"

The look is memorable too — a WiFi router sticking out of its head, Teletubby-esque, hence the Chinese nickname "Teletubby Robot." Pricing is approachable too: $30/hour (~200 RMB). In a market where real human cleaners charge $50-60/hour, that's genuinely competitive.

Three robot "employees" divide the work: Chelsea handles kitchens and bathrooms, Elon does recurring cleans (allegedly memorizing where things go after a few visits), Tony specializes in deep cleans — baseboards, tile grout, under the fridge, the real horrors. The website background loops the badass robot-exit-van shot on infinite repeat, oozing confidence.

Then you scroll to the comments, and one sentence brings everyone back to earth:

"All of this is remote-controlled. Pure. Human-Operated. Intelligence."

Wait — Is This Actually a Smart Move?

Friends, I'll be honest: reading "all remote-controlled," my heart sank. After all that, it's just a person wearing VR gear teleoperating the robot from somewhere? And that counts as AI?

But after calming down and thinking about it, I realized it's not that simple. Tau Robotics is a tiny 2024-founded company, under 10 people, that raised $3M Pre-seed last September and went radio silent. They're not scamming investors — the website doesn't trumpet "fully autonomous," even if it doesn't exactly advertise the teleoperation either. And there's one detail I have to praise: every single video plays at 1x speed.

Friends, you might not know this, but the overwhelming majority of humanoid robot home demos are shown at 2x speed minimum, with 4x needed to look halfway decent. That Tau dared show 1x video means they've already out-honested 90% of their peers.

The deeper logic is the data flywheel. Teleoperation isn't laziness; it's a strategy analogous to autonomous driving's "shadow mode" — get humans to teleoperate through real home scenarios first, collect massive operation data, then train autonomous models once there's enough data. 1X Neo took this approach last year, though nine months post-launch it still hasn't shipped at scale.

And building humanoid rather than wheeled has a first-principles reason: teleoperating a humanoid robot, operators can map movements directly using their own body intuition, which is far simpler than controlling a wheeled chassis. The humanoid form may not be optimal for "task efficiency," but it is optimal for "data collection efficiency."

Is Paying $30/Hour for a Human to Remotely Mop Your Floor Reasonable?

But here's the real question: will consumers pay $30/hour for a remote-controlled robot with a WiFi router on its head wandering around their home? The robot is always connected to the internet — that's a camera-equipped telepresence device roaming your house. It watches you wipe the table, watches you mop, sees the bills stuck on your fridge. How does privacy work?

One commenter put it well: "A humanoid machine walking around your house wiping tables and taking out trash while you sit on the couch watching... paying $30 to feel like an Athenian citizen for an hour doesn't sound half bad." Jokes aside, getting embodied AI into homes is genuinely harder than most people think. Even China's domestic robot-cleaning partnership between Zibianliang and 58 Tongcheng requires a real human cleaner standing next to the robot, watching. It's not that technology doesn't want to be autonomous — real home environments are just that complex.

A closing thought: I actually kind of admire Tau Robotics. Instead of making a flashy autonomous demo and raising a monster round to burn slowly, they chose an approach that looks "dumb" but is actually deployable — use teleoperation to run the service, charge customers real money, collect real data. "Pure human-operated intelligence" is a meme the internet gave them, but from another angle, it might be the most honest path in embodied AI startups today.

After all, in an era where every robot demo plays at 4x speed and every AI product brags about being "fully autonomous," a company that shows 1x video and gets the work done via human teleoperation first comes off as weirdly endearing.

That's all the roasting for today. More tomorrow.
